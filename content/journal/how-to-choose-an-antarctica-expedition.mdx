Every Antarctic voyage promises ice, penguins and a sense of being very far from everything — and every one delivers some version of it. The differences that matter are quieter: where the ship goes, how many people it carries, when it sails and what you are really paying for. Get those four decisions right and the rest tends to fall into place.

## Start with the route, not the ship

Most first-time travellers begin by browsing ships. We suggest starting with geography instead, because the route decides what you will see and how many days you will spend at sea.

### The Antarctic Peninsula

The classic voyage sails from Ushuaia across the Drake Passage to the Peninsula and back, usually over ten to twelve days. It offers the most departures, the widest choice of ships and the most accessible prices. For most people, this is the right first trip.

### Fly-cruise

If time is short or the Drake worries you, a fly-cruise from Punta Arenas to King George Island puts you in Antarctic waters within hours. It costs more per day and depends on flight weather, but a week can feel complete.

### South Georgia and the Falklands

Three weeks or so, with several sea days, and wildlife on a scale that is hard to describe: king penguin colonies stretching to the mountains, elephant seals on every beach, and Shackleton's grave at Grytviken. If you can give it the time, few voyages compare.

### Further and deeper

The Antarctic Circle, emperor penguin voyages and the Ross Sea are specialist trips. They reward experienced travellers, flexible expectations and bigger budgets — and the ice always has the final word.

## Ship size changes everything ashore

This is the decision most people underestimate. Under IAATO guidelines, ships carrying more than 500 passengers cannot land guests at all, and only a limited number of visitors may be ashore at a site at one time. On a ship of around 100 guests, almost everyone can be ashore together; on a ship of 190, landings run in rotations and each group's time is shorter.

> A smaller ship doesn't mean a lesser experience. Very often, it means more Antarctica and less waiting.

All of these limits are subject to change, so treat the specifics as a guide and verify current rules with partner operators and IAATO.

Larger expedition ships, in return, tend to offer more stability in the Drake, more public space and a wider range of cabins. Luxury ships combine small guest numbers with suites and fine dining, at a price.

## Choose your season deliberately

The Antarctic season runs roughly from November to March, and each part of it feels different:

- **November** — pristine snow, penguin courtship, fewer ships, colder days.
- **December and January** — the longest days, hatching chicks, the warmest weather and the highest demand.
- **February and March** — whale activity peaks, chicks fledge, and the light softens into real sunsets.

There is no wrong month, only different priorities. If whales matter most, sail late. If you dream of untouched snow, go early.

## Understand what the price includes

Fares are usually quoted per person in a shared or twin cabin and normally include meals, landings, guiding and lectures. Many operators add a parka to keep and boots on loan. What's usually not included:

1. Flights to Ushuaia or Punta Arenas
2. Hotel nights before and after the voyage
3. Mandatory travel, medical and evacuation insurance
4. Gratuities and optional activities such as kayaking or camping

When comparing two voyages, compare the total cost, not the headline fare. Inclusions vary by operator, so verify them before booking.

## Ask the questions brochures don't answer

A few that make a real difference:

- How many guests does the ship actually carry on this departure?
- How many days are realistically spent in Antarctica, not at sea?
- What happens if a fly-cruise flight is delayed?
- Are optional activities guaranteed, or subject to space and weather?
- What experience does the expedition team have in these waters?

Independent advice exists precisely for these questions. We don't operate ships, so our only interest is matching you with the voyage that fits — the right route, the right ship and the right moment in the season.

## The short version

Choose the route that matches your time and curiosity, the smallest ship you can comfortably afford, and the month that suits the wildlife you most want to see. Then let Antarctica do the rest.
