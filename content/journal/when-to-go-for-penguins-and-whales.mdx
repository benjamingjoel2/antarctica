The Antarctic summer is short, and life crowds into it. In barely five months, penguins court, nest, raise chicks and send them to sea, while whales arrive from the tropics to feed on swarms of krill. When you sail shapes which chapter of that story you see.

## The season at a glance

The expedition season runs roughly from late October to March. Every part of it is extraordinary, but each offers something distinct:

- **Late October to November** — courtship, nest-building, pristine snow
- **December to January** — hatching, chicks, the longest days
- **February to March** — fledging chicks and peak whale activity

## Early season: November

November is Antarctica waking up. Snow still blankets the landscape, sea ice lingers in the bays, and few footprints mark the shore.

### What you'll see

Penguins return to their colonies and begin courting and gathering stones for nests. Gentoo, chinstrap and Adélie colonies are lively and noisy. In South Georgia, elephant seal pups lie on the beaches, and king penguins are present in vast numbers year-round.

### Why go now

The scenery is at its most pristine, and the light is crisp. There are fewer ships, and early-season fares are sometimes lower.

### Emperor penguins

A handful of specialist voyages in late October and November attempt to reach emperor penguin colonies in the Weddell Sea, sometimes using helicopters. These are true expeditions, with no guarantee of success.

## High summer: December and January

### What you'll see

Penguin eggs hatch from around December, and by January colonies are full of downy grey chicks demanding food. Adults shuttle constantly between the sea and their young. Fur seals breed in South Georgia, and leopard seals patrol the edges of the colonies.

Whales start to appear in good numbers, especially humpbacks and minkes.

### Why go now

Daylight can last for twenty hours or more around the December solstice, and temperatures are among the warmest of the year. Sea ice is at its least by January, opening routes further south and into the Ross Sea.

### The catch

This is peak season. Holiday departures sell out early, and prices are at their highest.

> If you want to see penguin chicks at their most endearing, aim for late December through January.

## Late season: February and March

### What you'll see

Whale season peaks. Humpbacks gather in bays to feed, often bubble-netting close to the ship and zodiacs. Orcas and minke whales are frequently seen. Penguin chicks moult into their adult feathers and begin to fledge, taking their first awkward swims.

### Why go now

Retreating ice gives the best chance of crossing the Antarctic Circle. Sunsets and sunrises return, bringing long, golden light that photographers love.

### The catch

Colonies become muddier and quieter as chicks leave, and weather grows more changeable towards the end of the season.

## Matching the month to your priorities

1. **Pristine landscapes and courtship:** November
2. **Penguin chicks and long days:** December and January
3. **Whales and dramatic light:** February and March
4. **Emperor penguins:** late October to November, specialist voyages only
5. **King penguins in South Georgia:** any time in the season

## Watching responsibly

Whatever the month, wildlife comes first. Visitors are asked to keep at least 5 m (15 ft) from penguins and seals, and further from animals that show signs of disturbance. Whale-watching approaches follow strict guidelines, and expedition teams will always let animals approach on their own terms rather than chase them. These distances and rules should be verified with partner operators and IAATO before travel.

## A note on certainty

Wildlife timing shifts slightly from year to year and from site to site, influenced by sea ice and weather. The patterns above are reliable guides, not promises. Part of the magic of Antarctica is that it never quite follows the script.

Whichever chapter you choose, you'll witness one of the greatest wildlife spectacles on the planet.
